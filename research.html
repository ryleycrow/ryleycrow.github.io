Research

Amphibian Mast Cell Biology

Mast cells are best known and villainized for driving allergies in mammals, and the most understood mechanism for their activation is mediated through IgE. Although amphibians have mast cells, they lack IgE, which raises the question of how amphibian mast cells are activated. In the Grayfer lab, I study mast cells in Xenopus laevis to define what is conserved and what is unique between amphibian and mammalian mast cells. I compare activation pathways, including antibody-mediated activation through amphibian isotypes such as IgY, and antibody-independent activation through MRGPRs, venom peptides, and pathogen recognition. I also characterize what these cells release when they degranulate using proteomics, metabolomics, and RNA sequencing.

I am investigating how mast cells function in amphibian skin, where they contribute to protection against the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd), a pathogen devastating amphibian populations worldwide. Using cytokine enrichment, forced degranulation, and infection studies, I aim to define whether and how mast cells protect amphibians against fungal disease.

Immune Mechanisms in Non-Chordate Deuterostomes

I previously studied the immune system of the California purple sea urchin, Strongylocentrotus purpuratus, in Dr. Courtney Smith's lab. Sea urchins lack antibodies and rely entirely on innate immunity, yet they thrive in a microbe-rich marine environment. One of their key defenses is the SpTransformer (SpTrf) gene family, which is upregulated in response to immune challenge and extremely variable within and among sea urchins.

To test how these proteins function, I worked with recombinant SpTrf isoforms and evaluated their ability to act as opsonins and induce phagocytosis. We also found that soluble SpTrf proteins bind to specific sites on the phagocytes and modulate expression of the SpTrf gene family and other immune genes, revealing a mechanism by which the immune response is tuned to the level of the foreign threat.

This research reveals the distinct and sophisticated immune mechanisms in an invertebrate lacking the well studied adaptive immune system. Additionally, as deuterostomes, sea urchins help uncover the evolutionary story of vertebrate immune systems.
